What is vibe coding?

A way for non-technical users to create software or apps by describing what they want in natural language, while AI takes care of generating the code behind the scenes.

"There's a new kind of coding I call 'vibe coding', where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget the code even exists."

— Andrej Karpathy, Feb 2025

You talk to AI. AI writes code. You get a working tool.

The Ohio State University 2

Why vibe code?

  • Explore what technology can do — hands on, not theoretical
  • Turn repeated processes into tools you can share with colleagues
  • Customize software to your specific practice area or use case
  • Build things that don't exist yet because no developer knows your workflow

The key distinction

When you vibe code, AI plays two very different roles:

🤖 → 🔨

AI as the Builder

You describe what you want. AI writes the code. This is the vibe coding part.

≠
📱

The Finished App

The app runs on its own. There is no AI inside it. It just follows the rules it was built with.

Think of it like hiring a carpenter to build a bookshelf. The carpenter leaves, and the bookshelf just holds books. It doesn't build more shelves.

The Ohio State University 4

What your app can & can't do

✓ Your app CAN

  • Follow a decision tree (if/then logic)
  • Calculate deadlines, fees, or scores
  • Look things up in data you provide
  • Display information based on user choices
  • Enforce rules and flag errors

✗ Your app CANNOT

  • Read and interpret a document
  • Summarize or paraphrase text
  • Answer open-ended questions
  • Make judgment calls
  • Do anything requiring "understanding"

📇 The index card test: If you could write every possible answer on index cards ahead of time, your app can handle it. If you'd need a smart person to figure out the answer — it can't.

The Ohio State University 5

Security & Deployment

  • Do not enter client data into vibe-coded apps — these tools are not vetted for confidentiality
  • Secure deployment may require IT help or technical know-how
  • Ethical duties still apply — no legal advice, maintain confidentiality, no misleading advertising
  • Treat vibe-coded tools as internal aids, not client-facing products (at least for now)

Steps to follow

The Ohio State University 7
1

Identify a pain point

  • Tasks you find yourself doing over and over again
  • Processes that slow you down or feel inefficient
  • Workflows where errors frequently occur
  • Situations where you thought "there has to be a better way"
  • Information you want to share in an engaging, interactive way

Remember the index card test: Your pain point needs a solution that follows predictable rules, not one that requires judgment or interpretation.

Example projects

Legal practice

A decision-tree tool that walks someone through what to do after being served

A deadline calculator based on federal or state court rules

Teaching

An interactive quiz on debt collection basics or civil procedure

A "spot the red flag" exercise for contract review

Personal life

A step-by-step Thanksgiving dinner timeline from a set of recipes

A checklist and packing tool for travel planning

All of these follow rules and logic — no AI needed at runtime.

3

Ask AI to help plan

  • Tell it what you want to build and how you want it to work
  • Tell it what tools and platform you have available
  • If you're not sure the platform can handle your idea, ask!

Pro tip: Describe the logic, not the magic. Say "if the user picks X, show Y" rather than "I want it to figure out the right answer." If you catch yourself saying "figure out" or "understand," your idea might need AI at runtime — and that's beyond what these platforms do out of the box.

The Ohio State University 11
4

Execute

  • Go to the prompt site — it will generate a starter prompt and factual resources for your topic
  • Paste the prompt into Gemini Canvas
  • Test it — click through, try different inputs
  • Don't worry about getting it perfect on the first try
The Ohio State University 12
5

Iterate

The first version is just the start. The real skill is the conversation you have with AI after that.

  • Ask for explanation — "What does this part of the code do?"
  • Consider security — "What best practices should I follow?"
  • Consider edge cases — "What could go wrong? What inputs haven't I thought of?"
  • Refine the UX — "Make it look more professional," "add a back button"
The Ohio State University 13

Things you can ask for next

Once your first version works, try asking Gemini to:

Look & feel "Make it look more professional and polished"
Navigation "Add a back button so users can change their answer"
Content depth "Add a short explanation after each answer so the user learns why"
Edge cases "What if someone picks [unusual input]? Handle that gracefully"
Progress "Add a score tracker" or "Show a progress bar"
Mobile "Make this work well on a phone screen"

Level up as you go

Level 1

Get it working

Generate your first version. Click through it. Does it do the basic thing you intended?

Level 2

Add depth

More branches, more scenarios, richer explanations. Make it actually teach something.

Level 3

Polish the UX

Professional look. Progress indicators, back buttons, better layout and colors.

Level 4

User test it

Have someone in your cluster try it cold. Fix whatever confuses them.

Everyone should reach Level 1. Levels 2–4 are stretch goals — go as far as you can.

Session plan

~2 min
Form clusters — groups of 4–6, sit together, agree on a topic
~3 min
Pick your mechanic — each person chooses individually, visit the prompt site for your starter prompt
~30–45 min
Build & iterate — get to Level 1, then keep leveling up. Raise your hand if stuck!
~10 min
Cluster share — everyone in your cluster tries each other's tools
~5 min
Whole-room share — a few volunteers demo (60 sec each): what you built, what was hard, what you'd do next
